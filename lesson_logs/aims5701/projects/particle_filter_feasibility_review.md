## 3. Corrected mathematical formulation

### What changing the route can cause

In the minimal experiment use one population of functionally equivalent eligible writes. Measure work in seconds at one normalised bottleneck, which can perform one second of work per elapsed second. Let $r$ be the probability a request goes to Java, $\lambda$ the offered writes per second, $d_R$ and $d_J$ the mean synchronous DB work per Ruby and Java write, and $d_P$ the mean **additional DB work** for claiming and marking one Java event. The outbox insert is already in $d_J$. Let $b$ be the background arrival rate times the mean work per background job, in work-seconds per second. Then the eventual work offered per second is

$$
\rho(r)=b+\lambda[(1-r)d_R+r(d_J+d_P)],\qquad
\rho(r_1)-\rho(r_0)=\lambda(r_1-r_0)(d_J+d_P-d_R).
$$

This is work accounting under one event per Java commit, no dropped work and no retries. It counts the relay work incurred by a cohort even if execution falls in a later window. Instantaneous busy time and finite-horizon breach probability also depend on the work already in the queue. If only a fraction of writes generate events, multiply $d_P$ by that eligibility probability and stratify the workload. A language change alone implies no sign for the difference. Variance, visit ordering and locks can also change tails at equal mean work.

**Illustrative arithmetic, not a measurement.** At 40 writes/s, suppose Ruby uses 10 ms, Java uses 12 ms including the insert, and the relay uses another 6 ms. At $b=0.45$, total offered work is 0.882 at 10% and 1.01 at 50%. Sustaining the higher load exceeds capacity. At $b=0.65$, even 0% gives 1.05. Neither example determines how soon a finite-horizon SLO fails. An already full queue can breach after demand has fallen. Exposure can create a second, purely statistical effect. If independent events each miss with probability 0.01, the chance that at least one misses rises from about 9.6% for 10 events to 63.4% for 100. This is why E2 uses a common population of offered writes.

### State, transitions and what is observable

Define $X_t$ as **expected background work offered per second** during measurement interval $t$. The evaluator knows the generating rate and mean job size. Realised arrivals fluctuate around this target. CPU utilisation is a different quantity. A continuously busy server looks 100% busy with either a small or a growing queue. At low load a long interval with no delayed outbox completions is compatible with multiple background demands. Where query-level CPU or wait data exists, the premise of using only an indirect sensor changes. Aurora measures active sessions on CPU and sessions waiting, and exposes dimensions including SQL and host. [AWS, Database load](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/USER_PerfInsights.Overview.ActiveSessions.html)

A small **forecaster assumption** is persistent background demand below one unit on its own. Write $X_t=1/(1+e^{-z_t})$ and assume

$$
z_{t+1}=m+\phi(z_t-m)+\sigma\epsilon_{t+1},\qquad \epsilon_{t+1}\sim N(0,1).
$$

Here $z_t$ is the log odds of the demand fraction, $m$ its long-run centre on that scale, $0\leq\phi<1$ persistence per window, $\sigma$ the innovation scale, and each $\epsilon$ an independent standard normal draw. Use the stationary normal prior for $z_0$, with mean $m$ and variance $\sigma^2/(1-\phi^2)$, or declare a separate warm-up prior. Freeze these choices in development. The model has no separate shock mechanism and bounds background demand below capacity. Fit persistence and noise, if estimated, by predictive likelihood of permitted development telemetry or declare a plausible range and report prior sensitivity. Do not regress on the evaluator’s hidden rate labels. Routing affects the *foreground jobs*, so it drops out of the background transition under the frozen independent-disturbance assumption. Incoming demand and realised routed counts through the forecast origin remain known inputs.

The full generator state also contains the ordered DB jobs and their service requirements, the service remaining on the active job, the FIFO list of pending commit times including any in-flight event, and the publisher phase and remaining acknowledgement time. Retain workload phase or the next scheduled exogenous event where the process requires it. Two systems with the same $X_t$ and pending count can have a just-started long query or a nearly completed one. They will forecast differently. Exponential service permits some residuals to be integrated out within a job class. Heavy-tailed jobs or a batch with fixed duration generally require residual work or elapsed phase. Queue count alone cannot recover oldest age. These variables make the *simulator* Markov given its input process. Only the background rate is the scientific inference target.

**A minimal independent forecasting model.** Use a FIFO queue for foreground DB visits at effective speed $1-X_t$, with short service requirements estimated in isolated Ruby, Java and relay trials. Carry unfinished work across windows. Keep a single FIFO relay with commit times and a fixed nominal sink law. This replaces explicit background-job scheduling in the generator with an average loss of capacity. It can miss head-of-line blocking, which gives E4 a precise failure mechanism. The per-particle queue is reconstructed from the allowed history starting from a common prior or empty warm-up. Initialising it from the evaluator’s true outstanding DB jobs would invalidate E2. State plainly which design quantities are supplied or calibrated and vary them in a held-out regime.

### A usable observation likelihood

At each fixed observation time record pending count, age of the oldest pending commit, and median commit-to-mark delay among events marked in the interval. Also record the median Java write latency among writes completed in the same interval, with its sample count and a no-completions code. For conventional monitoring, retain the raw per-request latencies available at that time. A no-pending age and a no-completions delay have distinct categorical codes. Preserve sampling counts and eligibility flags. A pending event of age 12 s has already survived 12 s without a mark. Its completed delay is right-censored. A blocked Java transaction creates no outbox row until commit, so an empty queue can coexist with very slow writes.

One modest bootstrap likelihood avoids fitting four correlated continuous densities. Before testing, coarsen each metric into a few engineering bands, including the empty codes. Let $v$ be the joint vector of bands generated by one particle’s simulated interval, and $y$ the observed vector. Use the explicit observation law

$$
L(y\mid v)=(1-\eta)\,\mathbf 1\{y=v\}+\eta\,h(y).
$$

The indicator is one if the entire vector agrees. The constant $0<\eta<1$ allows a discrepancy between surrogate and measured bands. The normalised distribution $h$ gives every physically feasible joint category some probability, for example a smoothed frequency table from permitted development telemetry. This is an assumed coarse observation model. Its discrepancy term accounts for imperfect reporting/model agreement. Random arrivals, service times and $X_t$ innovations already supply process variation. For an ablation, project the vectors onto the retained coordinates and **sum** $h$ over the omitted ones. Do the same with eligibility information that would reveal a removed metric. This yields comparable likelihoods without multiplying the marginal probabilities of age, count and delay. Exact matching can starve particles even with smoothing. Record the fraction of intervals with any match and hold out whole traces to check the resulting uncertainty.

Each interval, propagate the hidden demand and queue, multiply the previous particle weight by $L$, and normalise across particles. With $N$ particles and normalised weights $w_i$, the effective sample size is $1/\sum_{i=1}^N w_i^2$. Systematically resample below a predeclared threshold such as $N/2$. High effective sample size when *no* particle matched means the observation taught the filter nothing. Check contraction from the prior and agreement across independent filter runs as well. Write latency is hidden from the outbox-only filter, including at initialisation. Conversely, avoid feeding commit IDs or exact backlog into the latency-only arm.

### Risk at a candidate share

Define $\mathcal I_t$ as telemetry and inputs actually available by time $t$ for that model. Let $B_{t,H}$ mean the predeclared write SLO breaches within the next $H$ seconds. Let $M$ denote the fitted forecaster, its capacity and service assumptions, and its forecast law for incoming requests and adjacent work. Estimate

$$
R_M(r)=P_M(B_{t,H}=1\mid\mathcal I_t,\operatorname{do}(U_{(t,t+H]}=r)),\qquad
\Delta R_M=R_M(r_{\rm proposed})-R_M(r_{\rm current}).
$$

The intervention sets one routing probability for the entire future horizon. For each share, average future breach indicators over the updated particles **and fresh future randomness**. Draw future foreground traffic from the same declared forecast law across shares. Sample unknown future background under its law. A realised future batch onset is unavailable at decision time. Report absolute risk at the current share alongside the increment, in probability points. Neither sign nor size is guaranteed. The simulator supports this intervention because route assignment is manipulable there. Transporting its answer to a production migration would require measured demands, workload mix and an independently validated contention model.

## 4. Minimal defensible experiment

### Simulator and checks before inference

Use a seeded SimPy event loop, one first-come-first-served DB resource of capacity one and a FIFO event store. Generate requests using a piecewise-constant Poisson arrival rate. Sample a route independently for each request. Both versions visit the DB. One atomic Java write completion creates one timestamped event. A single worker claims it using the DB, releases the DB while awaiting the simulated sink, then reacquires it to mark completion. Emit commit, acknowledgement and mark times separately. A mark can be late even after successful delivery. Background queries use the **same** DB queue with their own arrival and service distributions. Start with exponentially distributed per-class DB work and a higher mean for background jobs. Vary their arrival intensity independently of the route. Use a short nominal acknowledgement time, then change only that time or the relay rate for the publisher control.

SimPy supplies event scheduling, timeouts, resource waiting and release, and stores. The researcher supplies service demand, queue discipline, atomic effects, acknowledgement behaviour, timestamps and interventions. An exclusive slot gives the running long query sole access until it finishes. A time-shared CPU lets short jobs make progress concurrently. More exclusive slots introduce a parallel-worker model, and a preemptive resource supplies interrupts rather than automatic fair sharing. Label the result a shared serial DB bottleneck. A literal CPU claim would require a processor-sharing scheduler that tracks remaining work and reallocates capacity on arrival/departure, or measurements justifying a surrogate. Connection pools and row locks require separate resources and mechanisms. Neither is needed for this contention question. [SimPy resource primitives](https://simpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api_reference/simpy.resources.html) · [Istio weighted traffic shifting](https://istio.io/latest/docs/tasks/traffic-management/traffic-shifting/)

Validate in the following order.

1. Disconnect the relay and run one stationary job class. For independent Poisson arrivals of rate $a$, independent exponential service of rate $s>a$, and a single FCFS server, mean arrival-to-completion time is $1/(s-a)$. With general independent service time $S$ and $aE[S]<1$, mean **waiting** time is $aE[S^2]/(2(1-aE[S]))$. Here $E$ means expectation. Test at light and near-capacity load, with warm-up, independent replications and Monte Carlo intervals. Increasing the service-time second moment at fixed mean should increase waiting in this test. Feedback visits from the relay violate the independent-Poisson assumptions of the second formula. Use it for the isolated check. [MIT M/M/1 notes](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-854-introduction-to-manufacturing-systems-fall-2016/927056a1af54772a587fd84ad4951e71_MIT2_854F16_Mm1Queue.pdf) · [Bertsekas and Gallager, Delay Models in Data Networks](https://web.mit.edu/dimitrib/www/Queueing_Data_Nets.pdf)
2. Verify pending at end equals pending at start plus committed events minus marked events, with in-flight events counted once. Check commit ≤ claim ≤ ack ≤ mark, zero events on all-Ruby traffic, routed proportions within sampling error, and identical histories under deterministic replay. Never hold the DB token through the sink wait. Verify that a known test job spanning a forecast cut retains its remaining work.
3. **Route placebo.** Use identical synchronous work on both routes. Give Ruby an equivalent asynchronous dummy visit sequence and worker/sink behaviour in this control only, so the route label changes no physical scheduling. A single common work stream with Java-labelled rows exposed to telemetry suffices. Under matched random draws, pooled write outcomes should coincide exactly, even though the number of visible Java events changes. Equal *mean* DB demand alone is a weaker check because different visit patterns can alter tails.
4. **Mechanism pilot.** At matched histories measure write outcomes under hold and increase with (i) ordinary background demand, (ii) increasing adjacent work, (iii) background overload where both shares fail, and (iv) a slow relay with a healthy DB. Display write outcomes alongside pending age. If the scientific contrast exists only after threshold shopping or only in the relay control, stop before PF tuning. Record the originally proposed parameters and the result of each failed check.

### Endpoint, four experiments and fair scoring

**Primary endpoint proposed to resolve the frozen “write or delivery” choice.** Use all offered eligible writes, Ruby and Java. As a concrete pilot contract, use 30 s non-overlapping windows, $H=120$ s, a write deadline of 250 ms, and a breach if more than 5% of writes with deadlines in any future window are unfinished at their deadline. For the first window, include only arrivals after the decision. Empty windows have no eligible writes and cannot trigger a rate breach. Record denominators. These numbers describe a synthetic service target and may be set once on engineering grounds before opening test data. Timestamp the breach when the window closes. Alongside the aggregate, show lateness by route so a severe low-volume Java regression is visible. This endpoint avoids treating unfinished slow work as healthy or creating extra breach opportunities merely by adding Java events. Report delivery timeliness separately if time allows, using ack for delivery and mark for processing. Sensor thresholds and the evaluation endpoint serve different purposes.

| Frozen experiment | Minimum comparison | What would disconfirm the proposed benefit |
|---|---|---|
| E1. Healthy versus gradual contention | Generate calm and gradually increasing background job rates with independently varied onset and traffic mix. Compare the posterior mean/interval for offered background work against evaluator truth, a persistence prior, and a queue estimate. Report absolute error in work-seconds/s and empirical coverage of 90% intervals by regime. | A small state error copied from a persistent prior, or wide intervals offering no improvement over the prior, provides little evidence of sensor information. |
| E2. Weighted-rollout forecasts | Histories use 5%, 10% and 25%, with decision origins fixed in advance. At 25%, branch to 25%, 35% and 60%. Score a short step and a distant step separately, including held-out capacity or job-size settings. Reserve both higher shares from forecast fitting and tuning. | Excellent held-share predictions and miscalibrated 60% forecasts expose extrapolation failure. A flat risk curve is a valid result in a saturated or uncongested regime. |
| E3. Sensor comparisons | Same PF and forecast engine with outbox only, Java write latency only, and both. Repeat with each outbox metric individually omitted from the outbox arm. Use common histories, timestamps, forecast candidates and alarm budgets. Score against the future **write** breach. | If the best latency-only method alerts earlier at equal false alarms, or the combined arm gains no held-out decision value, withdraw the outbox advantage. |
| E4. Abrupt change and aliasing | Use an independent abrupt background change unseen by the transition model, plus the frozen fixed-capacity publisher control. Include matched prefixes followed by “shock” and “no shock”. | Predictions on identical prefixes must agree before the shock is revealed. A method that predicts which copy will suffer the shock has leaked information. A DB-pressure alarm under publisher-only delay is a false attribution. Report recovery time after onset separately. |

**Why warning could go either way.** A persistent slowdown can accumulate across many relay visits while successful synchronous writes still pass their threshold. Conversely, a write must commit before it creates a row, oldest age takes time to grow, and completed delay can look better as slow events disappear from the sample. Batching, polling frequency, Kafka acknowledgements, retries or a stuck publisher can produce an indistinguishable queue history. Decomposing claim wait and sink wait would help diagnosis if instrumented. They stay evaluator diagnostics for this experiment. Equalise wall-clock sampling and data availability between methods. Give the latency comparator normal access to sparse or missing windows and record the event counts at low shares. Benchmark the operator’s pooled write-SLO alarm as well as a Java-specific latency alarm, with the same cadence and false-alarm allowance. Use a latency-only predictive comparator so a win over a reactive alarm cannot be misread as a sensor win.

**Truth and independence.** Generate independent development, calibration and sealed test histories using disjoint root seeds. Reserve at least one entire generator setting as well. All descendants of a history stay together. At each cut, replay the exact seed to the cut or fork a serialisable physical state. Preserve residual work already drawn. Give the evaluator independent future draws across continuation replicates. Pair the candidate shares within a replicate using common exogenous arrivals, per-request route uniforms, service draws keyed by job identity, and background innovations. A single global random sequence consumed in different orders breaks this pairing. Forecasters get neither the snapshot nor these random streams. Apply each candidate from the same cut without conditioning on what a gate chose to promote.

Start with 30 independent test histories per core scenario and 50 continuation pairs per cut as **pilot precision targets**, subject to measured runtime. At probability 0.5, 50 independent continuations give a per-state standard error of about 0.071, so a small increment at one cut will be unresolved. Add independent histories before adding many overlapping windows. Use extra continuations where a narrow near-threshold decision matters, with the budget rule fixed before testing. The evaluator’s branch frequency estimates risk given its full state. Two identical visible histories can have different full states. Expect a calibrated observational forecaster to average such cases appropriately.

**Scores and decision rule.** Use mean Brier score, the mean squared difference between predicted probability and the realised zero-or-one outcome, across held-out continuations with equal weight per root and share. Report paired differences against the strongest baseline with a 95% bootstrap interval resampling whole histories, keeping their arms and forks together. Show branch-frequency differences for $\Delta R$, prediction error by share and regime, and reliability bins with counts, observed breach rates and uncertainty. A single aggregate reliability curve can conceal a failure near saturation. Report the no-history base-rate predictor as a reference. Brier score rewards honest probabilities. [Gneiting and Raftery, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)

For a worked decision rule, charge four units for promoting into a breach and one for holding when the proposed share would have been safe, with zero loss otherwise. Hold at predicted risk 0.20 or higher. This illustrates the consequences of a declared teaching cost. Report missed unsafe promotions per breached continuation, false holds per safe continuation, their denominators and mean realised cost. Separately calibrate alarm thresholds on healthy development histories to the same probability of any false alarm per episode. Compare first pre-breach alarm times for the **same candidate and continuation**, at the same decision cadence. Run all monitors offline so a hold cannot censor the subsequent outcome. For each method give fraction warned at least one window early, fraction missed, and paired lead times among jointly warned episodes. Show false alarms on non-breach episodes and misses alongside the lead-time distribution. Comparing only detected cases rewards selective silence.

**Leakage and extrapolation audit.** Seal thresholds, priors, likelihood bins, sensor arms, horizons, observation cutoffs and decision costs before evaluation. Fit only to telemetry that the corresponding method could access. Trace IDs, intervention labels for future disturbances, residual query times, future percentiles and completed delays recorded after the cut are evaluator-only. Use public isolated service calibrations for all mechanistic methods. A deliberately broad calibration sweep that already exposes the knee at 60% changes E2 into interpolation across scenarios and must be labelled as such. Near idle, a smaller server with cheaper background work and a larger server with more competing work may fit the same observed delays. Forecast both plausible calibrations at 60%. If they give opposite promote/hold decisions, report sensitivity or an indeterminate recommendation. Increasing the particle count would only estimate each model’s conflicting answer more precisely.

## 5. Evidence for and against using particle filtering

**Particles are a reasonable candidate. The frozen plan supplies no evidence that they are necessary.** The strongest case is near the capacity boundary during *persistent, gradually varying* background load. The same sparse telemetry can leave substantial probability on either side of the knee. Propagating both possibilities through an intervention can give a better breach probability than plugging a single smoothed estimate into a nonlinear queue. Even then, numerical integration on a small grid may produce the same answer more cheaply. These are hypotheses to test on a generator fixed independently of the PF. [Arulampalam et al., A Tutorial on Particle Filters](https://abbeel.berkeley.edu/cs287-fa12/optreadings/Arulampalam_etal_2002.pdf)

| Comparator | What to give it | What the comparison can establish |
|---|---|---|
| Reactive threshold | Current age/backlog or conventional write-latency alarm. Calibrate the episode false-alarm rate on the same development cases. | Timeliness and decision cost of warning over standard practice. A deterministic alarm need not invent a probability for a Brier comparison. |
| Moving average or trend | Trailing level and slope over a frozen window, observed volumes, current and proposed shares. For a probability, calibrate a simple logistic forecast on development outcomes at allowed shares. Include a level-only version if the slope is unstable. | Whether routine smoothing plus knowledge of the ramp explains the gain. Extrapolating a fitted share coefficient has the same support limits as E2. |
| Queue-only forecast | Same foreground service assumptions, nominal relay, workload forecast, sensor mask and starting-history budget as the PF. Match the last few sensor summaries to the closest constant-pressure setting in a small surrogate sweep. Propagate that point estimate through the same stochastic queue forecaster. | Whether tracking a *distribution* over pressure adds value beyond queue physics and a reasonable estimate of current headroom. Give this baseline future service/arrival randomness too. |
| HMM or scalar grid | Use a small low/medium/high pressure HMM with the forward recursion (Bayes update summed over all regimes). A finer deterministic grid can resolve a one-dimensional continuous posterior. Condition emissions on permitted past queue summaries. A comparison claiming numerical equivalence must share the transition, likelihood and queue-memory approximation across algorithms. | An exact finite-state calculation has no Monte Carlo error in that model. In a small finite-state validation case with queue state observed or enumerated, PF estimates should converge to the forward calculation. A grid over pressure alone is insufficient for an exact comparison when queue memory is unobserved. |
| Kalman, extended Kalman or simple smoothing | A linear Gaussian observation/transition model suits approximately smooth latency well below saturation. An EKF locally linearises a differentiable observation function and represents one Gaussian uncertainty region. | These are credible alternatives in the smooth regime. Empty-window categories, censoring, one-sided queues and a posterior straddling a knee make a single Gaussian less attractive. They do not prove multimodality or establish that a PF beats a grid. Discuss them without implementing another family. |

**Where the PF should fail.** With zero Java traffic throughout the observed history, an initially empty outbox and no Java commits, the three outbox observations are identical at every background load. With matched forecast inputs, the outbox-only posterior should remain its prior. Similarly, no history can predict the realisation of an independent shock more precisely than its hazard. In an informative but stationary low-dimensional regime the queue baseline or forward recursion may tie or win on both score and runtime. Report runtime per update and per candidate forecast, variation over PF seeds, and the effect of doubling particles on a small frozen subset. If only the oracle-parameter version succeeds, credit the assumed service model. If both model-based methods beat thresholds but their paired score difference is inconclusive, credit the predictive queue model and leave SMC’s incremental value unresolved.

## 6. Literature positioning

The closest precedents address the two halves of the frozen question separately. This was a targeted primary-source search across server state and service-rate estimation, sequential deployment tests, risk-based ramps, ML release analysis, chaos diagnosis, outbox metrics, and incremental migration performance. It includes author papers, proceedings, and first-party engineering documentation. It provides a bound on the evidence reviewed here.

| Prior work | Verified result and bearing on this proposal |
|---|---|
| **Closest filtering analogue** | Ruuskanen and Cervin (2018) estimated internal server state from arrival and response information with an event-based PF in a simulated queue. Their preliminary performance comparison was against stationary analytic estimators. Hidden server-state inference from indirect telemetry already has a direct precedent. The outbox, competing workload and intervention are different experimental choices. [Internal Server State Estimation Using Event-Based Particle Filtering](https://lup.lub.lu.se/search/files/49609380/wip_ebccsp_18.pdf) |
| **Predictive queue model** | Ruuskanen and Cervin (2022) extracted a fluid model from distributed microservice observations and evaluated predictions under perturbations. Their instrumentation is richer than a pending-event queue. It strengthens the case for a queue-based predictive baseline. [Distributed Online Extraction of a Fluid Model for Microservice Applications](https://portal.research.lu.se/en/publications/distributed-online-extraction-of-a-fluid-model-for-microservice-a/) |
| **Closest rollout decision analogue** | Zhao, Liu and Deb (2019) combine sequential monitoring with automated ramp-up and compare time-, power- and Bayesian risk-based schedules. Their Bayesian rule concerns losses from exposing more users to an uncertain treatment effect. Here increasing exposure can itself change the treatment and control response distributions through a congested shared DB. Predictive Bayesian ramp sizing already exists. [Safely and Quickly Deploying New Features with a Staged Rollout Framework](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10493) |

| **Automated canary and ML release analysis** | Google’s Canary Analysis Service analyses metrics during a partial deployment. Li et al.’s *Gandalf* (NSDI 2020) uses correlation, ranking and a binary impact classifier on rollout fault signals in Azure. These are precedents for automated gates and ML-assisted decisions on observed rollout effects. Neither source establishes calibrated extrapolation across a DB saturation boundary. [Google, Canary Analysis Service](https://research.google/pubs/canary-analysis-service/) · [Gandalf](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/li) |

(2025) use Chaos Mesh for fault injection and a dynamic Bayesian network for incident inference after anomaly detection. Here an injected disturbance can test causal specificity, but simulation gives no production fault frequencies. [Automating Chaos Experiments in Production](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04648) · [Anomaly Detection and Root Cause Analysis in Cloud-Native Environments](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2025.3565220) |
| **Outbox mechanics and operational signals** | Richardson describes the atomic write and separate relay, including duplicate publication. Debezium illustrates log-based change-data capture, a different relay from the planned claim/mark worker. Their sensitivity, specificity and lead time for DB contention remain unverified here. | **Change impact in incremental migrations** | Faustino et al. (2022) measure performance and effort at successive migration stages. This is adjacent empirical evidence for resource differences after decomposition. It supplies neither a Ruby-versus-Java service-demand estimate nor an online strangler routing-risk model. [Stepwise Migration of a Monolith to a Microservices Architecture](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.07226) |

**Credible MSc contribution.** Show how well an indirect sensor distinguishes persistent DB competition from a slow relay, and quantify what knowing the posterior changes about a specific next-share decision. A figure pairing two nearly identical outbox histories with different write risks, plus a held-out table where a simple method ties or beats SMC, would constitute a substantive result. I could not verify that this sensor identifies Aurora CPU, that it generally leads request latency, or that an identical joint study already exists. There is no basis here for claiming a new filtering algorithm, a first rollout gate, or production validity.

## 7. Implementation feasibility

As of 25 November, the plan’s first milestone has passed. The following budget assumes no working simulator, protects the 3 December assessment, and treats the stated 30–45 hours conservatively as the **whole human budget**, including interpretation and presentation. Agent coding time does not remove the need to audit independence, service semantics and the student’s explanation of the filter.

At 35–40 hours, spend the extra 5–10 hours on checkpoint/likelihood debugging, a same-model grid or finite-HMM check and more independent histories. Hold up to five further hours for failed runs or revisions. The main technical risks are restoring the **physical** state at a fork, losing all informative particle matches at low shares and spending the budget on correlated continuations. Deterministic prefix replay is adequate in a small model. Coarse bins and short horizons keep computation bounded. If a usable likelihood cannot be demonstrated on development cases by 8 December, report its information limits and compare the simplest finite-regime version allowed by the frozen fallback. Keep the PF as a tested method if the submission makes any claim about SMC.

**Scope cuts in order.** Remove animation, online gate integration, full-rollout time, optional particle sweeps and a second delivery endpoint. Keep one nominal sink, one DB discipline, one core workload mix, one horizon, one nearby and one distant promotion, one abrupt shift and one publisher control. Reuse trajectories for the sensor masks. If time is still short, prioritise complete branch truth and interval estimates over a dense routing curve. The three-share curve, E1–E4, queue baseline, latency-only comparator, controls and written interpretation are the minimum evidence for this approval. Dropping the route intervention would turn the project into a different question.

## 8. Final recommendation

**I would approve this proposal for Fundamentals of AI with the modifications above.** A student eight weeks into formal AI can demonstrate Bayesian prediction and updating, uncertainty under partial observability, and a meaningful comparison with simpler inference. Prior engineering experience helps construct valid controls. The hardest assessed work should be explaining why two histories imply different posterior risks, and when the data cannot distinguish them.

**What changes my verdict.** Continue after the pilot if the isolated queue passes its check, the route placebo has the expected null result, and a documented, plausible difference in per-route work produces a measurable nearby or distant routing contrast in at least one predeclared regime. At the December checkpoint require a reproducible held-out branch forecast and a working route-aware queue baseline. A PF loss, a late outbox sensor or a large uncertainty interval are reasons to publish a negative result. If a routing effect appears only through extra event-count exposure, if the estimator needs hidden generator state, or if credible branch evaluation cannot fit before the deadline, I would decline the full predictive-gate proposal. The pilot would then have answered the decision that matters now.
